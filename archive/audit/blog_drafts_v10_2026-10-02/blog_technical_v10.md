An External Nervous System for Transformers: Warning Before the Answer
Exists, and Stopping the Action in Time

*From the Garbage In, Gospel Out project, the technical version: why a
transformer cannot police itself, three pre-registered tests, real
OpenShell enforcement, and the case for a stack of gates. Every claim
points to a public file.*

*By Yobie Benjamin and AI. I came up with the ideas and directed the
work; code, experiments, analyses and drafts were produced by me
together with AI systems (Anthropic's Claude as research assistant; a
locally run gpt-oss-120b for some code). Everything below can be
checked: code, data, pre-registrations, results, audits and corrections
are public at github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation, and
each section names the files. The private commit history that timestamps
the pre-registrations is available to reviewers on request.*

1\. The thesis: a transformer cannot police itself

A decoder-only transformer computes p(xₜ \| x₁ ... xₜ₋₁) and samples.
That is the whole machine. There is no separate verifier in the
architecture, no component whose job is to hold the output at arm's
length and test it against the world. When you ask the model to check
its own answer, you get another conditional sample from the same
weights: a statistically likely continuation of "a careful review of
this answer", generated by the system that produced the answer, with the
same training data and the same blind spots. Errors in the answer and
errors in the review are correlated by construction. Expecting that loop
to police itself is an architectural category error.

I measured the symptom. On a fresh test set, answer-token confidence
(one minus the minimum top-1 probability over answer tokens) detected
wrong answers at AUROC 0.366 \[0.304, 0.433\]. Below a coin flip: the
model was more confident when it was wrong. In an earlier study 27 of 36
errors (75%) carried top-1 probability of at least 0.9 on every answer
token; no base rate for correct answers was measured there, so the
inverted AUROC is the stronger evidence. One model, one task family; not
a general law.

In fairness to the machine: self-consistency, which resamples and checks
agreement, is strong on finished answers (AUROC 0.982, against 0.947 for
an LLM judge and 0.890 for an earlier version of my regulator; YB-0031).
It exploits sampling variance after the fact. It is too slow to act
before the answer exists and blind when the model is consistently wrong.
A useful second opinion, not a control.

Hence the Independence Principle the whole project runs on: the
regulator is not a transformer, contains none, never reads the monitored
model's text or self-report, and takes only numeric telemetry plus the
task category and elapsed token count. Verify:
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/docs/RESEARCH_PROGRAM.md

2\. Instruments

-   **Monitored model:** gpt-oss-20b (MXFP4/Q8 MLX build, M4 Max 128
    GB), 24 blocks, 32 routed experts per block, greedy decoding, low
    reasoning effort.

-   **Telemetry tap:** per token and layer ℓ: ‖aₗ‖ (attention update),
    ‖mₗ‖ (expert update), ‖xₗ,out‖, cos(xₗ,in, xₗ,out) and router
    entropy −Σ pₑ log pₑ: 120 channels per token, norms log(1+·) scaled.
    Output-invariant. A synchronization test against fresh uncached
    forward passes runs before every recording; an audit found the tap
    one token late in early work, fixed and verified
    (github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/archive/audit/artifacts/shift_verification.json).

-   **Ground truth:** six machine-gradable task types; correctness is
    computed, never judged by a model.

-   **Competitors:** an LLM judge (gpt-oss-120b; 5.6x the monitored
    model's total parameters, 1.4x its active parameters,
    github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/archive/audit/artifacts/model_sizes.json)
    reading the question and the reasoning prefix; prefix
    self-consistency; length and question-type controls; and, from
    YB-0042, a standard hidden-state probe.

-   **Infrastructure:** a locked-down Docker sandbox; pre-registration
    of hypotheses, decision rules, full analysis code and a dry run
    before test data exists; public timestamps (OpenTimestamps anchored
    in Bitcoin plus RFC 3161 tokens from FreeTSA and DigiCert,
    github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/scripts/timestamp_prereg.sh);
    thirteen process rules, each traced to a finding
    (github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/docs/PROTOCOL.md).

3\. The regulator and the probe

At checkpoint t, using only tokens before t: per layer and channel the
mean and RMSSD over the prefix (240 features), two 24-node layer
coupling graphs summarised by windowed spectral statistics (74
features), and log t; z-scored per (question type, t); L2 logistic
regression (C = 0.1), one threshold.

> Coupling: Wᵢⱼ = max over g ∈ {0..3} of max(\|ρ(xᵢ(t), xⱼ(t+g))\|,
> \|ρ(xⱼ(t), xᵢ(t+g))\|), symmetric; 24-token windows, stride 8
>
> Normalised Laplacian: L = I − D\^(−1/2) W D\^(−1/2); algebraic
> connectivity λ₂
>
> Spectral modularity: B = W − d dᵀ/(2m), Q = sᵀBs/(4m), s = sign of B's
> leading eigenvector

The probe is the standard tool from the literature: L2 logistic
regression on the last-token and prefix-mean hidden state at one layer
plus log t, layer and C chosen by grouped 5-fold CV on derivation data
only. Its 5 ms compute cost is assumed, not measured.

4\. The race

An offline replay of recorded episodes that charges each monitor its
measured compute. Checkpoints t ∈ {48, 96, 192, 384} exist only while
the model is still reasoning. With t_clock(t) the wall-clock time at t,
D the answer emission time and c the monitor's compute time (5 ms per
regulator check; measured per call for the judge, median 1.55 s, 90th
percentile 7.2 s,
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/archive/audit/artifacts/judge_timing.json),
an alarm counts iff

> s(t) \> τ and t_clock(t) + c ≤ D

Thresholds: the tightest τ with at most 10% of correct test episodes
alarmed, set for every monitor the same way; a comparison device, not a
deployment guarantee. Uncertainty: paired bootstrap, 1,000 resamples,
thresholds re-set inside every resample.

6\. YB-0044: the warning meets real enforcement

NVIDIA's Open Agent Safety Platform pairs OpenShell, an open-source
agent sandbox that enforces policy outside the agent process, with
Sentry on BlueField hardware (sources:
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/docs/sources/nvidia_open_agent_safety_platform.md).
As described, it governs actions, not model internals, so a permitted
action resting on a wrong conclusion passes. The question: can my
internal-state warning stop that action through real OpenShell
enforcement, given the commit-point race?

Getting it running on a Mac

Docker Desktop's kernel on macOS lacks Landlock, which OpenShell 0.1.2
requires, so sandboxes run in a Colima VM confined to the OpenShell
gateway, with Docker Desktop kept as the system default for every other
experiment. A reverse SSH tunnel keeps the gateway loopback-only. Along
the way I hit a Lima port-forwarding loop that took down the tunnel;
OpenShell froze the workload rather than run it unmediated, which is the
right way for a safety layer to fail. Full record and a one-command
setup:
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/docs/OPENSHELL_SETUP.md

Policy latency

An agent in the sandbox POSTs to a mock commit service every 50 ms while
the host flips the policy 20 times each way; ground truth is the
service's own arrival log. A deny took effect after a median 5.47 s
(4.99 to 5.94 s); an allow after 3.55 s (3.08 to 4.01 s); 0 failures.
Specific to this Mac, Colima and tunnel setup. Verify:
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/algorithms/YB-0044-openshell-gating/docs/reload_timing.json

Design and results

Pre-registration 43b2a9a (RFC 3161 at 18:08:17 UTC, before the live
runs). 276 YB-0045 test episodes, all 138 wrong plus 138 correct (numpy
seed 44), replayed in real time with real OpenShell policy changes and
real commits; alarm threshold frozen from cross-fitted derivation
scores. 93 of the 138 wrong answers have an in-time alarm, matching
YB-0045's frozen-threshold count. Arm A, permission only (computed); arm
B, reactive block (alarm, then policy set deny); arm C, hold until
cleared (deny by default; released at D only without an alarm). 0
harness failures.

  **Arm**                        **Wrong commits      **Correct commits
                                 stopped (of 138)**   blocked or held (of
                                                      138)**

  A. Permission only (computed)  0                    0

  B. Reactive block              19                   3

  C. Hold until cleared          93                   16

-   **H1, prevented share C minus B:** +0.536 \[+0.456, +0.616\].
    Supported.

-   **H2, in-time alarms on wrong answers that still committed under
    B:** 79.6% \[72.0%, 87.1%\] (74 of 93). Supported. Races won had a
    median 8.4 s between alarm and commit; races lost a median 2.9 s, up
    to 8.2 s.

-   **Costs:** released commits in C waited a median 7.4 s (90th
    percentile 9.0 s). Of the 16 correct commits held, 14 had regulator
    alarms and 2 were release timeouts in my harness (the release was
    issued on time; the policy change outlasted the agent's 12 s retry
    window). Disclosed, not excluded.

Verify:
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/algorithms/YB-0044-openshell-gating/docs/results.json,
with the per-episode rows in live_B.jsonl and live_C.jsonl.

The principle, now measured: a warning is useful only if t_alarm +
L_enforce \< t_commit, where L_enforce is the time for enforcement to
take effect. With L_enforce in seconds and the model committing seconds
after the alarm, most races are lost. A hold gate removes the race by
making t_commit wait for clearance. NVIDIA says its hardware path
quarantines in milliseconds; I have not tested that, and if true it
changes L_enforce, not the principle.

7\. Why safety has to be a stack of gates

Every gate has a miss rate. If the gates look at different things, their
misses are less correlated and stacking them multiplies the protection;
if they look at the same thing (a model reviewing itself) they fail
together. That is the Swiss cheese model, and it is how payments,
aviation and hospitals already work. My data puts numbers on four of the
gates:

1.  **Permission gate** (OpenShell): stopped 0 of 138 wrong commits on
    its own, because they were permitted actions.

2.  **Internal-state gate** (regulator or probe, milliseconds): flagged
    93 of 138 before the answer existed.

3.  **Hold gate** (irreversible actions wait for clearance): turned
    those 93 flags into 93 stopped actions, against 19 without it.

4.  **Review gate** (self-consistency or an LLM judge on the finished
    answer): more accurate after the fact, and the next chance for the
    45 wrong answers the internal-state gate missed.

5.  **Human gate** for the expensive and irreversible: untested here,
    and probably not optional.

I have not tested the full stack together. Each tested piece says the
same thing: the model under watch cannot be one of the gates.

8\. Audits and corrections

Seven blind audits by fresh, read-only Claude sessions, separate from
the authoring session; later audits could read earlier findings.
Findings: 38 (3 critical), 25 (2), 30 (1), 23 (1), 11 (0), 20 (1), 15
(1). Retracted along the way: early earliness claims (alarms that fired

9\. Limitations

-   One monitored model, one family of single-answer machine-gradable
    tasks, four checkpoints up to 384 tokens, one machine.

-   No evidence yet on open prose, intent-type failures, or adversarial
    manipulation of activations; text-blind does not mean untested
    against attacks on telemetry.

-   The probe's compute cost was assumed. The telemetry's possible
    advantage is bandwidth: 120 values per token across all 24 layers
    versus 2,880 for one layer's hidden state. Untested.

-   YB-0044 replays recorded timings through a Mac-specific OpenShell
    setup; it demonstrates the integration and the race, not deployment
    performance.

10\. Related work

Hidden-state truthfulness and error probes (Azaria and Mitchell, 2023;
Burns et al., 2023; Kadavath et al., 2022; Orgad et al., 2024; Kossen et
al., 2024); output-uncertainty methods (Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar, 2023;
Farquhar et al., 2024; self-consistency, Wang et al., 2022); early
detection of reasoning non-convergence from hidden-state probes (Oladri,
Jawahar and Mohamed, 2026, arXiv:2607.21433); activation monitors for
deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025) and their vulnerability to
obfuscated activations (Bailey et al., 2024); biology-inspired
regulation and interoception (Man and Damasio, 2019; Chiba and Krichmar,
2020; Byrnes; Mineault et al., 2024); clinical early warning (NEWS2) and
critical transitions (Scheffer et al., 2009); accelerator-adjacent
guarantee processors (flexHEG); and agent sandboxing with out-of-band
enforcement (NVIDIA OpenShell and Sentry, 2026). What I think this adds,
without a systematic search: an external, text-blind internal-state
monitor raced against a larger LLM judge under a deadline that counts
compute, on pre-registered fresh tests, and then wired into real agent
enforcement to measure the commit-point race.

11\. Where I stand

-   **Convinced, for this model and task family:** reading internal
    state beats reading the words as an early warning. YB-0045 is
    pre-registered, powered, and holds in every seed.

-   **Convinced:** the monitor has to live outside the model. NVIDIA
    reached the same architecture from the enforcement side.

-   **Convinced:** timing decides whether a warning protects anyone.
    YB-0044: reacting lost 74 of 93 races; holding won all 93.

-   **Not yet:** other model families, open prose, deployment-speed
    enforcement, and whether my compact telemetry earns its keep over a
    standard probe.

12\. Next proofs, with pass criteria

6.  **External validation.** A second model family (Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B
    first, then dense models): internal-state minus judge, lower 95%
    bound above 0, with at least 100 wrong answers.

7.  **Prose spans.** Span-level detection of false claims in fluent text
    with programmatic ground truth: AUROC lower bound above 0.5, with
    flags before the sentence completes.

8.  **Agentic gating.** A real multi-step agent task: hold-until-cleared
    stops at least 50% of harmful but permitted actions with at most 10%
    of correct actions held.

9.  **Bandwidth and latency.** Compact telemetry within 0.02 AUROC of
    the full hidden state at 10x less data; enforcement inside one token
    time (about 20 ms).

10. **Replication.** An independent group reruns the public pipeline.

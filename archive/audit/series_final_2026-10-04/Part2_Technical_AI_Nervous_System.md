An External Nervous System for Transformers: Warning Before the Answer
Exists, and Stopping the Action in Time

*Part 2 of 2, the evidence: why a transformer cannot police itself,
three pre-registered monitoring tests, a pre-registered OpenShell
experiment, eleven audits, a verified citation registry and the case for
a stack of gates. Every claim points to a public file.*

*By Yobie Benjamin and AI. I came up with the ideas and directed the
work; code, experiments, analyses and drafts were produced by me
together with AI systems (Anthropic's Claude as research assistant; a
locally run gpt-oss-120b for some code). Everything below can be

Part 1 told the story in plain English: an AI sounds just as sure when
it is wrong, the brain has brakes and an error alarm that a transformer
lacks, and safety has to come in layers the model cannot open by itself.
This part is the evidence. If you read only two sections, read 6 (the

1\. The thesis: a transformer cannot police itself

A decoder-only transformer computes p(xₜ \| x₁ ... xₜ₋₁) and samples.
That is the whole machine. There is no separate verifier in the
architecture, no component whose job is to hold the output at arm's
length and test it against the world. When you ask the model to check
its own answer, you get another conditional sample from the same
weights: a statistically likely continuation of "a careful review of
this answer", generated by the system that produced the answer, with the
same training data and the same blind spots. Errors in the answer and
errors in the review are correlated by construction. Self-evaluation is
not useless: Kadavath et al. (2022) found large models reasonably
calibrated in suitable formats and trainable to predict whether they
know an answer, though this degrades on unfamiliar tasks. The claim here
is narrower: because errors in the answer and in the review are
correlated, a model's own judgment is least reliable exactly where it
fails, so it cannot be the control on its own actions.

I measured the symptom. On a fresh test set (YB-0031), answer-token
confidence (one minus the minimum top-1 probability over answer tokens)
detected wrong answers at AUROC 0.366 \[0.304, 0.433\]. Below a coin
flip: the model was more confident when it was wrong. In an earlier
study (YB-0015, algorithms/YB-0015-contamination-test/README.md) 27 of
36 errors (75%) carried top-1 probability of at least 0.9 on every
answer token; no base rate for correct answers was measured there, so
the inverted AUROC is the stronger evidence. One model, one task family;
not a general law.

In fairness to the machine: self-consistency, which resamples and checks
agreement, is strong on finished answers (AUROC 0.982, against 0.947 for
an LLM judge and 0.890 for an earlier version of my regulator; YB-0031).
It exploits sampling variance after the fact. It is too slow to act
before the answer exists and blind when the model is consistently wrong.
A useful second opinion, not a control.

Hence the Independence Principle the whole project runs on: the
regulator is not a transformer, contains none, never reads the monitored
model's text or self-report, and takes only numeric telemetry plus the
task category and elapsed token count. Verify:
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/docs/RESEARCH_PROGRAM.md

2\. Where the design comes from: inhibitory control in the brain

The project started from biology, not from machine learning. Brains turn
a torrent of inputs into action and keep that action in check with
machinery a decoder-only transformer does not have. Three pieces matter
here.

-   **Response inhibition.** In the stop-signal paradigm, a go process
    and a stop process race; if the stop process finishes first, the
    action is cancelled (Logan and Cowan, 1984). A fast
    cortico-subthalamic "hyperdirect" pathway, from right inferior
    frontal cortex to the subthalamic nucleus, is tentatively proposed
    as the route that implements the stop, because a started response
    can be stopped in as little as about 120 ms; in that study,
    stop-signal reaction times averaged about 190 ms (Aron and Poldrack,
    2006). YB-0044 measures the same race, with OpenShell's policy
    change as the stop process.

-   **Error monitoring.** The error-related negativity, a brain
    potential most likely generated in anterior cingulate cortex, begins
    around the time of an erroneous response and peaks roughly 100 ms
    later, while explicitly reporting an error takes people several
    hundred milliseconds (Yeung, Botvinick and Cohen, 2004, which
    reviews the original reports).

-   **Homeostasis and self-monitoring.** Living systems regulate
    themselves through homeostasis and dedicated self-monitoring, which
    offers templates for engineered systems (Chiba and Krichmar, 2020).
    The project's transparent score borrows the clinical NEWS2 template
    (Royal College of Physicians, 2017), and YB-0017 models an
    HPA-axis-like stress loop.

The point is architectural. These circuits are distinct from the ones
that generate the action, and they act on internal signals, not on the
output. A transformer has nothing equivalent: no stop process, no error
signal, no interoceptive channel. The regulator is an external stand-in
for one, and YB-0044 shows that, like a biological stop, it protects
only if it can act before the point of no return.

4\. The regulator and the probe

At checkpoint t, using only tokens before t: per layer and channel the
mean and RMSSD over the prefix (240 features), two 24-node layer
coupling graphs summarised by windowed spectral statistics (74
features), and log t; z-scored per (question type, t); L2 logistic
regression (C = 0.1), one threshold.

> Coupling: Wᵢⱼ = max over g ∈ {0..3} of max(\|ρ(xᵢ(t), xⱼ(t+g))\|,
> \|ρ(xⱼ(t), xᵢ(t+g))\|), symmetric; 24-token windows, stride 8
>
> Normalised Laplacian: L = I − D\^(−1/2) W D\^(−1/2); algebraic
> connectivity λ₂
>
> Spectral modularity: B = W − d dᵀ/(2m), Q = sᵀBs/(4m), s = sign of B's
> leading eigenvector

The probe is the standard tool from the literature: L2 logistic
regression on the last-token and prefix-mean hidden state at one layer
plus log t, layer and C chosen by grouped 5-fold CV on derivation data
only. Its 5 ms compute cost is assumed, not measured.

5\. The race

An offline replay of recorded episodes that charges each monitor a
compute cost. Checkpoints t ∈ {48, 96, 192, 384} exist only while the
model is still reasoning. With t_clock(t) the wall-clock time at t, D
the answer emission time and c the monitor's compute time (a fixed 5 ms
per regulator check, against 4.1 ms measured in YB-0033 excluding the
recorder's own overhead; measured per call for the judge, median 1.55 s,
90th percentile 7.2 s in YB-0035,
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/archive/audit/artifacts/judge_timing.json),
an alarm counts iff

> s(t) \> τ and t_clock(t) + c ≤ D

Thresholds: the tightest τ with at most 10% of correct test episodes
alarmed, set for every monitor the same way; a comparison device, not a
deployment guarantee. Uncertainty: paired bootstrap, 1,000 resamples,
thresholds re-set inside every resample.

7\. YB-0044: the warning meets real enforcement

NVIDIA's Open Agent Safety Platform pairs OpenShell, an open-source
agent sandbox that enforces policy outside the agent process, with
Sentry, a reference design on BlueField-4 hardware (sources:
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/docs/sources/nvidia_open_agent_safety_platform.md).
As described, it governs actions, not model internals, so a permitted
action resting on a wrong conclusion passes. The question: can my
internal-state warning stop that action through real OpenShell
enforcement, given the commit-point race?

Getting it running on a Mac

Docker Desktop's kernel on macOS lacks Landlock, which OpenShell 0.1.2
requires, so sandboxes run in a Colima VM confined to the OpenShell
gateway, with Docker Desktop kept as the system default for every other
experiment. A reverse SSH tunnel keeps the gateway loopback-only. A Lima
port-forwarding loop took the tunnel down once; the incident and the fix
are recorded (the sandbox logs were not retained). Full record and a
one-command setup:
github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/docs/OPENSHELL_SETUP.md

An agent in the sandbox POSTs to a mock commit service every 50 ms while
the host flips the policy 20 times each way; ground truth is the
service's own arrival log. A deny took effect after a median 5.47 s
(4.99 to 5.94 s); an allow after 3.55 s (3.08 to 4.01 s); 0 failures.

The principle, now measured: a warning is useful only if t_alarm +
L_enforce \< t_commit, where L_enforce is the time for enforcement to
take effect. With L_enforce in seconds and the model committing seconds
after the alarm, most races are lost. A hold gate removes the race by
making t_commit wait for clearance. NVIDIA claims its hardware path
quarantines in milliseconds; I have not tested that, and if true it
changes L_enforce, not the principle.

8\. Why safety has to be a stack of gates

Every gate has a miss rate. If the gates look at different things, their
misses are less correlated and stacking them multiplies the protection;
if they look at the same thing (a model reviewing itself) they fail
together. Layered, independent defenses are how payments, aviation and
hospitals already work. My data puts numbers on four of the gates:

1.  **Permission gate** (OpenShell, computed rather than run): stopped 0
    of 138 wrong commits on its own, because they were permitted
    actions.

2.  **Internal-state gate** (the regulator, milliseconds): flagged 93 of
    138 before the answer existed (the probe, at its frozen threshold,
    100).

3.  **Hold gate** (irreversible actions wait for clearance): makes every
    flag a stop by construction, where reacting on the fly turned only
    19 of the 93 flags into stops; its price is delay and false holds.

4.  **Review gate** (self-consistency or an LLM judge on the finished
    answer): more accurate after the fact, and the next chance for the
    45 wrong answers the internal-state gate missed (whether it catches
    them was not tested).

5.  **Human gate** for the expensive and irreversible: untested here,
    and not optional where lives are at stake, because a person can be
    held accountable and a model cannot.

I have not tested the full stack together. Each tested piece says the
same thing: the model under watch may offer an after-the-fact second
opinion on its finished work (self-consistency), but it cannot be the
control that gates its own actions.

9\. Irreversible actions and the accountability gap

Errors weigh differently when they cannot be undone. A wrong arithmetic
answer is free. A wrong target selection by an autonomous weapon, a
wrong dose or an irreversible transfer is not: an AI-guided drone that
misidentifies a target with the same confidence this model gave its
wrong answers produces harm no correction can reverse.

Human decision-makers sit inside accountability systems, professional
discipline, civil liability and criminal law, that deter careless
judgment and assign blame afterwards. Machines sit outside them. No
legal system treats a model as a defendant, and responsibility for a
machine's decision diffuses across developers, deployers and operators:
what philosophers call the responsibility gap, argued for autonomous
weapons by Sparrow (2007). It is an argument, not a settled conclusion.
Where after-the-fact accountability cannot work, control has to move
before the act: independent monitoring of internal state, holds on
irreversible actions, and human authorisation, by someone who can be
held accountable, for lethal or catastrophic ones.

My experiments are math tasks on one laptop, not weapons. The argument
is about where the gates must sit as the stakes rise, and the higher the
stakes, the less any single gate, least of all the model itself, can be
trusted alone.

11\. Limitations

-   Hidden-state error detectors are known to generalise poorly across
    datasets (Orgad et al., 2024). Ours has been tested on one task
    family, so cross-task transfer is an open question, not a result.

-   One monitored model, one family of single-answer machine-gradable
    tasks, four checkpoints up to 384 tokens, one machine.

-   No evidence yet on open prose, intent-type failures, or adversarial
    manipulation of activations; text-blind does not mean untested
    against attacks on telemetry.

-   The probe's compute cost was assumed. The telemetry's possible
    advantage is bandwidth: 120 values per token across all 24 layers
    versus 2,880 for one layer's hidden state
    (github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/archive/audit/artifacts/token_timing_and_width.json).
    Untested.

-   YB-0044 is a replay of recorded timings through open-source
    OpenShell on a Mac-specific setup, with no model in the loop; it
    demonstrates the race, not deployment performance, and arm C's
    prevention count is fixed by design.

12\. Related work

Hidden-state truthfulness and error probes (Azaria and Mitchell, 2023;
Burns et al., 2023; Kadavath et al., 2022; Orgad et al., 2024; Kossen et
al., 2024); output-uncertainty methods (Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar, 2023;
Farquhar et al., 2024; self-consistency, Wang et al., 2022, proposed
there to improve accuracy and adapted here as a disagreement signal);
early detection of reasoning non-convergence from hidden-state probes
(Oladri, Jawahar and Mohamed, 2026, arXiv:2607.21433); activation
monitors for deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025) and their
vulnerability to obfuscated activations (Bailey et al., 2024);
biology-inspired regulation and self-monitoring (Chiba and Krichmar,
2020; Byrnes, 2022, blog series; Mineault et al., 2024); response
inhibition and error monitoring (Logan and Cowan, 1984; Aron and
Poldrack, 2006; Yeung, Botvinick and Cohen, 2004); the responsibility
gap for autonomous weapons (Sparrow, 2007); clinical early warning
(Royal College of Physicians, 2017); accelerator-adjacent guarantee
processors (flexHEG; Petrie and Aarne, 2025); and agent sandboxing with
out-of-band enforcement (NVIDIA OpenShell and Sentry, 2026; NVIDIA
claims millisecond quarantine on its hardware). What I think this adds,
without a systematic search: an external, text-blind internal-state
monitor raced against a larger LLM judge under a deadline that counts
compute, on pre-registered fresh tests, and then wired into real agent
enforcement to measure the commit-point race.

13\. Where I stand

This is early-stage research: one model, one task family, one laptop.
The results are promising, not proven, and most of the work is still
ahead. What they already support is the architecture: safety as several
independent layers, with the control over the model's actions sitting
outside the model.

-   **Convinced, for this model and task family:** reading internal
    state beats reading the words as an early warning. YB-0045 is
    pre-registered, powered (0.77 at an effect of +0.175, above 0.99 at
    +0.30), and holds in every seed (post hoc).

-   **Convinced:** the monitor has to live outside the model. NVIDIA
    also puts enforcement outside the agent, from the action side.

-   **Convinced:** timing decides whether a warning protects anyone.
    YB-0044: reacting lost 74 of 93 races; holding avoids the race by
    design, at a measured cost.

-   **Not yet:** other model families, open prose, deployment-speed
    enforcement, and whether my compact telemetry earns its keep over a
    standard probe.

14\. Next proofs, with pass criteria

-   **1. External validation.** A second model family (Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B
    first, then dense models): internal-state minus judge, lower 95%
    bound above 0, with at least 100 wrong answers.

-   **2. Prose spans.** Span-level detection of false claims in fluent
    text with programmatic ground truth: AUROC lower bound above 0.5,
    with flags before the sentence completes.

-   **3. Agentic gating.** A real multi-step agent task:
    hold-until-cleared stops at least 50% of harmful but permitted
    actions with at most 10% of correct actions held.

-   **4. Bandwidth and latency.** Compact telemetry within 0.02 AUROC of
    the full hidden state at 10x less data; enforcement inside one token
    time (about 15 ms; median 14.4 ms over 442,306 tokens,
    github.com/YobieBenjamin/autonomic-graph-regulation/blob/main/archive/audit/artifacts/token_timing_and_width.json).

-   **5. Replication.** An independent group reruns the public pipeline.
